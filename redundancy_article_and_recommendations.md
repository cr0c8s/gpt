# Career: Redundant But Not Out

## Full Recognized Text of the Article

**Source:** Career magazine article — "Losing your job may seem like the end. But it could be a great new start." By Jay Andrews.

**This month:** Redundant but not out - When two loves make one job - Breaking the promotion barrier - Career notebook.

---

Counsellors liken redundancy to bereavement, with its cycle of disbelief, anger and grief. And even if you do hear of plans to cut back, your own redundancy is still shocking.

"I understand people had to go," says Meredith, who lost her job in a graphics company earlier this year. "But I was devastated. I came home and cried. After all the hours I'd put in, I felt I had failed. I could hardly face colleagues, let alone family and friends."

Understandable feelings, but unjustified. When you walk out of that appalling meeting with a boss, remember you are not alone. Above all, it's not your fault. For the last three years we've been in the biggest recession since the Thirties: Department of Employment figures inform us that around 100,000 people a month have been facing redundancy. Blame whom you like for an economy that sees one in nine workers unemployed — blame the industry you're in — but don't blame yourself.

### When It Happens

"You should keep your head and listen carefully," says Sheila Cane, co-author of the book *Putting Redundancy Behind You* (Kogan Page, £8.99).

- **Listen** to what the company offers you. Ask for terms of redundancy, when they want you to go, and for time, at least a couple of days, in which to confirm arrangements.

- **Check your legal entitlements.** Go to your union or professional body, or the nearest Citizens Advice Bureau. Be sure if it is redundancy the company is meeting its obligations. After two years' employment you qualify for statutory redundancy. Also, examine both your personal contract and company practice. If you can't agree your legal rights, you can take your case to an industrial tribunal (find out how at your local Jobcentre) or consult a solicitor specialising in employment law (see Yellow Pages and check fees before giving a solicitor your case). If the company is going into liquidation, expect to be advised by the Insolvency Practitioner.

- **Negotiate everything** you feel you are entitled to. Take in a union representative to help you negotiate if necessary. Apart from pay, you might look for a deal on the company car or the computer you use, home health insurance at company terms; the period of notice. Ask the company if it is providing out-placement advice and use it.

- **Be professional about it.** "I gritted my teeth and did a good job organising a large trade show I wasn't even going to be at," says Jane Browning, who recently lost her corporate communications job. "It paid off. I got great references, and three months later they gave me a freelance contract to make a promotional video."

- **Get an open letter** from the company confirming you have been made redundant, not fired, and giving you a brief reference.

### The First Few Weeks

- **File your P45.** You will need it for your next job and to claim any unemployment benefit. You must sign on as unemployed: even if you don't get benefits, you do get National Insurance credits. Phone your benefits office (under Employment Service Offices in the phone book) and make an appointment with a client adviser who will check benefits available to you. They range from unemployment benefit, for which you qualify regardless of your redundancy package, to needs-based income support and relief from mortgage interest payments.

- **Work out a budget.** Include all expenses. Be sensible: look at the implications of giving up the car or dropping home contents insurance.

- **Watch your spending.** Don't blow your redundancy payment. Whichever magazine also warns not to rush into converting your money or engaging a financial adviser. Consult at least three before investing.

- **Inform building societies, banks, hire purchase companies etc.** if you are going to run into debt. Most have advisers to help work something out.

### Planning the Future

"The worst thing you can do," says Mick Hawkins, chairman of InterExec, a leading outplacement company, "is rush into a job search like a headless chicken."

- **Evaluate yourself:** others at work and outside it; what you enjoy doing, what your skills are, where you'd like to live and the lifestyle you desire. Use the list to analyse what you want from your next job.

- **Identify careers** which match your requirements. Get more information on them from library books, newspapers, including the business pages and profiles. Consult trade and professional bodies (see Yellow Pages). Consider professional careers advice, though this is expensive.

- **Consider starting your own business,** but balance tempting independence against hard practicality. Read books on the subject. Research the market, get advice from your bank.

- **Use redundancy as an opportunity** to work out what you really want to do and achieve, which could be quite different from the expected field. "I wandered into my job as I expect most of us do," says Terence French, a former estate agent. "But I'd always loved history and art. So when I was laid off because my company was downsized, I had a big rethink and went for a career change. Now I organise and conduct specialist tours around southern England, and I just wish I'd done it before."

- **List your achievements** both at work and outside it.

### Rebuilding Your Confidence

- **Make an effort to keep yourself fit.** "I started swimming regularly," says Caroline Walsh, who used to manage a building society. "I now feel stronger, more capable." Others find exercise eased stress.

- **Cosset yourself.** Prepare delicious meals, spend time outdoors. See the exhibition you couldn't fit in before, join an adult education class. And watch your stress levels. Anger is healthy; sleepless nights and regular hangovers are not. Exercise releases tension, so do some every day.

- **Set yourself small daily targets** — physical or mental. It could be walking a mile, reading a chapter of a book, or shampooing the carpets.

- **Accept you no longer have a job.** Don't hide it from friends and family: if you live a lie, they can't offer comfort. Talk things through, but recognise when you're living in the past rather than preparing for the future, which must be your priority now.

- **Catalogue your strengths.** Ask close friends/family to describe them too. No, it isn't very British, but you can disguise it as an exercise. It will give you a clearer picture of yourself.

### Keeping Yourself Going

Once collapse, of not having work any longer, gives you a reason to get up. Impose a timetable as soon as you can. Sheila Cane suggests you divide your day into blocks of activities. These might include time with friends / working on plans for the future / sorting out your home.

- **Create a workspace at home** and stock it for the job hunt: stationery, files, stamps, a fax and photocopying service. Keep a diary to plan and log days, appointments and phone calls. File research and copies of letters you've sent, note calls and contacts.

- **On your CV you needn't mention redundancy.** "It will be clear from your finish dates," says Lillian Bonett of Manpower, Britain's largest employment services company, "but underline constructive things you're doing, such as acquiring new skills, a personal project, especially if you've been out of work a while."

### Where to Look

- **Scan the vacancies** in local and national newspapers (at no cost to you in libraries). Subscribe to trade publications; if you don't know which ones, call up the personnel department of a company in your desired field and ask where they advertise; ask your professional body or union.

- **Visit your local Jobcentre** and ask them to notify you of specific openings. After six months' unemployment, they offer Restart courses and Jobclub membership.

- **Contact recruitment agencies;** research them — at least call the switchboard and ask for an information magazine or brochure. Then write to the relevant person — the personnel manager say. Tell them why you want to work there, what you could offer the company, request a phone discussion. Then follow it up.

- **Think network.** Don't ask outright for a job, it puts your contacts in an embarrassing position. Instead, seek advice from friends and former colleagues, consult them over your career plans. Ask them to refer you to people who can help.

- **When you get an interview, be positive.** They'll only ask why you left your last company if they're interested in you. Prepare an answer you are comfortable with, such as "The company was restructuring." If asked, be prepared to say why, but it won't be an inquisition, just a test of your judgment. Leave it at that.

## List of Recommendations on Dealing with Redundancy

Based on the full article content, here is a structured list of recommendations organized by stage:

### Stage 1: Immediate Response (Day of Redundancy)

1. **Stay calm and listen carefully** — do not react emotionally in the meeting. Focus on understanding what the company is offering.
2. **Request time to process** — ask for at least two days before confirming any arrangements.
3. **Ask for written terms** — get the redundancy terms in writing, including financial package, notice period, and any additional benefits.
4. **Do not blame yourself** — redundancy is a business decision driven by economic conditions, not a reflection of your performance or worth.
5. **Remember you are not alone** — tens of thousands face redundancy every month; it is a systemic economic issue.

### Stage 2: Protecting Your Rights (First Week)

6. **Check your legal entitlements** — consult your union, professional body, or Citizens Advice Bureau immediately.
7. **Review your employment contract** — examine both statutory rights (after 2 years' employment you qualify for statutory redundancy pay) and any personal contract terms.
8. **Seek legal advice if needed** — if you cannot agree on your rights, take your case to an industrial tribunal or consult an employment law solicitor.
9. **Negotiate beyond basic pay** — negotiate for company car, computer equipment, continued health insurance, extended notice period, and outplacement support.
10. **Get a written reference letter** — obtain an open letter confirming redundancy (not dismissal) with a brief positive reference.

### Stage 3: Practical Administration (First Few Weeks)

11. **File your P45 immediately** — you will need it for your next employer and to claim benefits.
12. **Sign on as unemployed** — even if you don't qualify for unemployment benefit, you will receive National Insurance credits.
13. **Meet with a benefits adviser** — check all benefits available including unemployment benefit, income support, and mortgage interest relief.
14. **Create a detailed budget** — list all expenses and income, assess what you can realistically cut without long-term harm.
15. **Do not spend your redundancy payment impulsively** — avoid rushed investments. Consult at least three financial advisers before making any financial decisions.
16. **Inform creditors early** — contact banks, building societies, and hire purchase companies if you anticipate difficulty. Most have advisers to help restructure payments.

### Stage 4: Maintaining Professionalism (Notice Period)

17. **Continue to perform well during your notice period** — good work ethic during this time can lead to excellent references and even future freelance contracts.
18. **Maintain professional relationships** — leave on good terms with colleagues and management; these connections are valuable for networking.

### Stage 5: Emotional Recovery and Confidence Building

19. **Acknowledge the emotional impact** — recognise that feelings of disbelief, anger, and grief are normal and valid.
20. **Keep physically active** — regular exercise (swimming, walking, gym) reduces stress, improves mood, and builds a sense of capability.
21. **Treat yourself well** — prepare good meals, spend time outdoors, visit exhibitions, join adult education classes.
22. **Set small daily targets** — create achievable goals each day, whether physical (walking a mile) or mental (reading a chapter), to maintain momentum.
23. **Be honest with family and friends** — hiding your situation prevents others from offering support. Open communication is essential.
24. **Monitor stress levels** — anger is a healthy response, but chronic sleeplessness and excessive alcohol are warning signs that need addressing.
25. **Catalogue your strengths** — ask close friends and family to describe your qualities. This exercise provides a clearer, more objective self-image.

### Stage 6: Strategic Career Planning

26. **Do not rush into job searching** — avoid acting like a "headless chicken." Take time to evaluate before applying.
27. **Conduct a thorough self-evaluation** — assess your skills, interests, values, desired location, and lifestyle preferences.
28. **Use your evaluation to define criteria** — create a clear picture of what you want from your next role.
29. **Research alternative careers** — explore options through library books, newspapers, business pages, and trade/professional bodies.
30. **Consider a career change** — redundancy can be an opportunity to pursue a long-held passion or enter a completely different field.
31. **Explore self-employment** — research the market, seek bank advice, and read business startup guides, but balance enthusiasm with practical assessment.
32. **List all your achievements** — document professional and personal accomplishments to build your CV and interview confidence.

### Stage 7: Structured Job Search

33. **Create a dedicated workspace** — set up a home office with stationery, files, stamps, and access to fax/photocopying services.
34. **Keep a job search diary** — log all applications, appointments, phone calls, contacts, and follow-ups systematically.
35. **Update your CV strategically** — you do not need to mention redundancy explicitly; focus on constructive activities, new skills, and personal projects.
36. **Scan vacancies widely** — check local and national newspapers (free at libraries), subscribe to trade publications, ask companies in your target field where they advertise.
37. **Register with your local Jobcentre** — request notifications about specific openings. After six months, access Restart courses and Jobclub membership.
38. **Contact recruitment agencies** — research them first (request brochures), then write targeted letters to relevant personnel managers explaining what you can offer.
39. **Network strategically** — do not ask contacts directly for jobs (it creates awkwardness). Instead, seek career advice, discuss plans, and ask for referrals to people who can help.
40. **Impose a daily timetable** — divide your day into blocks: time with friends, career planning, home tasks. Structure prevents drift.

### Stage 8: Interview Preparation

41. **Be positive in interviews** — employers only ask about your departure if they are already interested in you.
42. **Prepare a comfortable explanation** — a simple statement like "the company was restructuring" is sufficient. Do not over-explain.
43. **Treat the question as a judgment test** — interviewers are assessing your composure and perspective, not interrogating you. Give a brief, confident answer and move on.

## Key Takeaways

| Aspect | Wrong Approach | Right Approach |
|--------|---------------|----------------|
| Emotional response | Blame yourself, feel ashamed | Recognise it's an economic event, not personal failure |
| Financial management | Spend redundancy pay immediately | Budget carefully, consult 3+ advisers before investing |
| Notice period | Check out mentally, do minimum | Perform excellently — it leads to references and future work |
| Job search timing | Panic-apply to everything instantly | Self-evaluate first, define what you want, then search strategically |
| Social response | Hide situation from everyone | Be open with family/friends — they are your support network |
| Networking | Ask contacts "Do you have a job for me?" | Seek advice, discuss plans, ask for referrals |
| CV strategy | Highlight redundancy | Focus on constructive activities and new skills |
| Interview approach | Over-explain departure defensively | Brief, confident: "Company restructured." Move on. |
| Daily routine | Stay in bed, lose structure | Impose timetable, set daily targets, create home workspace |
| Long-term thinking | Find same type of job immediately | Consider career change, self-employment, new passions |
